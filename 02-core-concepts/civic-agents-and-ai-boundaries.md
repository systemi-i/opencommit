# Civic Agents and AI Boundaries

Civic agents are software-mediated tools that help people or institutions navigate public processes. They may explain requirements, locate services, assemble documents, summarize evidence, prepare submissions, route cases, or help a person understand and exercise recourse.

ICSL does not treat those capabilities as institutional authority. Assistance and action are different roles, even when the same technical system performs both.

## Assistance, Evidence, and Authority

An AI system may extract a possible fact from a document, but the fact does not become durable institutional evidence until the protocol's acceptance process makes it so. It may draft reasons, but the authorized actor remains responsible for accepting an outcome. It may recommend a route, but the recommendation becomes a CommitmentPoint if it effectively controls access to a service.

Automated systems may hold delegated authority only where the protocol identifies a lawful delegation basis, the actor type is declared, and appropriate human recourse remains available. The presence of a model, score, or recommendation never supplies authority on its own.

## Shadow Binding

Some apparently preliminary activities have practical effects that rival formal decisions. Triage may prevent an application from reaching an official. A risk score may make approval practically impossible. An advisory finding may bind a later decision unless the official gives exceptional reasons to depart.

ICSL applies a shadow-binding test to those activities. If a routing, classification, or recommendation reliably creates a reliance-worthy constraint, it should be represented as a CommitmentPoint with its own gates and receipt rather than left as invisible runtime behavior.

## Value of the Boundary

Explicit boundaries make civic agents more useful, not less. An agent can confidently assist where the protocol permits, identify the point at which human or institutional acceptance is required, and help a person locate the source and recourse attached to an adverse act.

Independent advice tools outside an institution's trust boundary remain free to explain and advocate. ICSL governs the institution's acceptance of acts and evidence; it does not claim authority over public discussion or private assistance.
